    9.0.2: General Pre-Arrival Instructions/Interventions

    The following pre-arrival instructions/interventions should be applied to all calls:

    1. Always consider bystander and patient safety.
    2. Always consider the potential criticality of the following groups: pregnant women, paediatric patients, geriatric patients, persons with disability or frailty, and persons with infectious diseases.
    3. Always ensure good patient positioning.
    4. Always continue to assist or monitor the patient until help arrives.
    5. Always attempt to gather more details about the person’s symptoms, medical history, allergies, or any medications they are taking.
    6. Always call back if the condition of the patient deteriorates or changes.
    7. Always try to have someone meet the ambulance team.

    9.0.3: Mass Casualty/Major Incident

    Key Questions Dispatch Pre-Arrival Instructions/Interventions
    1. What is your exact location?
    2. What is the nature of the incident?
    3. Are there any immediate dangers to responders or bystanders?
    4. How many people are injured or affected?
    5. What types of injuries or medical conditions are the affected individuals experiencing?
    6. Are there any individuals who are trapped or unable to move?
    7. Are there any hazards or obstacles that could impede the response of emergency services?
    8. Have any bystanders provided medical assistance or interventions?
    9. Are any additional resources or personnel needed on site?
    10. Can you provide any additional information that may assist the emergency services in their response?

    Simultaneous ALS/BLS

    • Patients with a decreased level of consciousness.
    • Accident with crushing or penetrating injuries to the head, neck, torso, thigh.
    • Patients entrapped. Amputation other than fingers/toes.
    • Patients paralysed.
    • Patients with uncontrolled bleeding.
    • Multiple extremity fractures.
    • Femur (thigh) fractures.

    BLS Dispatch

    • Amputation/entrapment of fingers/toes.
    • Neck or back pains without critical symptoms.
    • Patients with controlled bleeding.
    • Patients with cuts, bumps, or bruises.
    • Involved in an accident, no major complaints.
    1. General pre-arrival instructions/interventions.
    2. Assess the situation and ensure you and other bystanders are safe. If there are any immediate dangers, such as a fire, gas leak, or ongoing violence, move to a safe location.
    3. If you have any medical training or experience, assist the injured individuals, e.g., applying pressure to wounds, performing CPR, or administering basic first aid.
    4. If you are unable to provide medical assistance, try to monitor the injured individuals and provide comfort and reassurance. Stay with them until emergency services arrive.
    5. Clear the path for emergency services vehicles to reach the scene, e.g., directing traffic or guiding responders to the location of the incident.

    9.1: Abdominal Pain

    Key Questions Dispatch Pre-Arrival Instructions/Interventions
    1. Is the pain due to an injury to the patient?
    2. How does the patient feel sitting up?
    3. Is the pain above or below the belly button?
      If the patient is female between 12 and 50 years:
    4. Could she be pregnant?
    5. Has there been vaginal bleeding? IF YES, how much? Has she said she felt dizzy?
    6. Has the patient vomited? IF YES, what does the vomit look like?
    7. Are the patient’s bowel movements black and tarry?
    8. Is the patient wearing a Medic Alert tag? IF YES, what does it say?
    9. Does the patient have Addison’s Disease, recent trauma or any other medical or surgical history?
    10. Has the patient recently travelled outside of the state or country? IF YES, Where? (Check ALERTS)

    Simultaneous ALS/BLS

    • Lower abdominal pain in a woman 12-50 years (if associated with dizziness, fainting, or heavy vaginal bleeding).
    • Vomiting blood (red/dark red) or coffee ground-like substance
    • Black tarry stool
    • Upper abdominal pain with a prior history of heart problems.
    • Abdominal pain with fainting or near fainting, patient over 50 years.
    • Pulsatile abdominal mass
    • Fainting/near fainting when sitting.(hypotension)
    • Pain with prior history of Addison’s disease or adrenal insufficiency. Symptoms of an Addison or “Adrenal” crisis include:
      - Severe vomiting and diarrhoea
      - Dehydration
      - Low blood pressure
      - Loss of consciousness
      If not treated, an Addison crisis can be fatal.

    BLS Dispatch

    • Pain with vomiting.
    • Flank pain (Kidney stone).
    • Abdominal (non-traumatic).
    • Pain unspecified.
    1. General pre-arrival instructions/interventions.
    2. Nothing to eat or drink.
    3. Monitor for SHOCK: skin cool and clammy or mottled, rapid shallow breathing, fatigue, altered mental state, dilated pupils.
